Our central proposal

The 6-Hour Work Policy

Same dignity and productivity, less working time, more time for family, health, learning and community.

A note on the 6-Hour Work Policy: this is Ekta NexCus's own advocacy proposal and awareness initiative — a vision for what Indian labour policy could become. It is not an enacted law, government scheme, or official policy of any state or central authority.
Why hours matter

The standard workday was never designed around people

The 8–9 hour (often longer, once commute and overtime are counted) workday is a legacy of industrial-era scheduling. Ekta NexCus argues it deserves the same scrutiny any inherited system gets when the world around it has changed.

Family time

Two to three hours returned daily to parenting, elder care, and simply being present at home.

Mental wellbeing

Shorter, well-bounded shifts reduce the chronic fatigue and burnout long hours tend to produce.

Youth employment

A four-shift day creates more entry points into work for a young, growing workforce.

Productivity

Well-rested workers on focused shifts are the basis of the "same productivity" claim — not a promise of more output for less pay.

Women's participation

Shorter, predictable hours remove one concrete barrier that pushes women out of formal work.

Community participation

Time for civic life, local governance, and the informal networks that hold communities together.

The mechanism

The four-shift concept

Instead of one long shift, the workday is proposed to split into four 6-hour shifts across a 24-hour cycle. In sectors where this fits — manufacturing, retail, logistics, services — this can mean more people employed overall, more scheduling flexibility for workers, and continuous operating hours for employers, without demanding a longer day from any one person.

This model doesn't fit every sector or role equally — agriculture, caregiving and many unorganised-sector jobs would need adapted approaches, which is part of the ongoing policy work.

The honest questions

Economic considerations

A serious proposal has to sit with its own trade-offs. Here's how we're thinking about the hardest questions, openly.

Will wages hold?

The proposal is built on "same pay for fewer hours," not a pay cut. Making that viable sector-by-sector, especially for low-margin unorganised-sector employers, is the central economic question we're researching.

Can small employers absorb this?

Any transition needs a phased approach and, likely, policy support — not a blanket mandate overnight. We don't have a finished answer here yet, and we say so.

What about output?

Global shorter-workweek pilots offer some evidence that focused hours can hold productivity steady in certain sectors — but results vary by industry, and we won't cite specific studies here without linking verified sources.

Who does this help first?

Our starting focus is the unorganised sector, where hours are least regulated and the case for reform is strongest — not formal-sector white-collar work.

FAQ

Common questions

Is this a law right now?

No. It is an Ekta NexCus advocacy proposal and awareness campaign. No government body has enacted it.

Does my employer have to follow this?

Not currently. We work with employers who choose to explore shorter-shift pilots voluntarily.

How can I support this?

Volunteer, mentor, share the idea in your own workplace, or bring your organisation on as a partner.
